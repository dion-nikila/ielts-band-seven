Tutor: Let us review your project on the new campus bicycle stands. What was your original question? Student: Whether adding stands increased cycling. We counted bicycles beside the library for two weeks before installation and two weeks afterwards. The counts were higher afterwards. Tutor: That is useful, but what else changed? Student: The later period had much drier weather. We should not attribute the whole increase to the stands.

Tutor: Did you count at the same time each day? Student: Yes, at eleven in the morning. We first planned nine, but a delivery vehicle often blocked the view then. The counts were done on weekdays only. We did not collect weekend data. Tutor: Include that limitation. Also distinguish parked bicycles from the number of individual cyclists. A bicycle could remain in the same place for several days.

Student: We also asked students whether they liked the stands. We collected eighty-four questionnaires. Tutor: How did you recruit people? Student: We approached cyclists near the library entrance. Tutor: Then you cannot treat that sample as representative of everyone on campus. You may have missed people who do not cycle because they need stands in another location. Put the recruitment method in the methods section.

Student: For the follow-up, should we buy automatic counters? Tutor: Not yet. First compare another location where no new stands were installed. Keep the observation times the same and record the weather. Use a spreadsheet for the field notes, but keep the original count sheets too. Submit your revised plan by Tuesday. The presentation is on Friday, so those are separate deadlines. In your conclusion, say what the evidence supports and what remains uncertain, rather than claiming that a higher count proves the policy caused more cycling.
